## Formatting & Structure Tips

A factory production worker's cover letter is screened by a plant HR coordinator or shift supervisor who is filling line positions fast, so keep it short, plain, and concrete — half a page to one page, two or three paragraphs, in a simple font (Arial or Calibri, 11-12pt). Skip graphics, columns, and color; manufacturing HR often pastes applications into an ATS or a basic web form where any styling collapses. Your goal is to read as dependable, safety-minded, and ready to start on the assigned shift.

Open by stating the production environment you've worked in — assembly line, machine operation, packaging, food/automotive/electronics manufacturing — and your shift flexibility (first, second, third, rotating, weekends, overtime), because availability is often the deciding factor. In the body, name the concrete skills supervisors scan for: line speeds you've kept pace with, units or pallets produced per shift, quality inspection and defect rates, machine setup and changeover, forklift or pallet-jack certification, and lean methods like 5S and Kaizen if you've practiced them. Lead with safety — years without a recordable incident, lockout/tagout and PPE compliance — since plants screen hard on it. Mirror the exact equipment and certifications from the posting, and state plainly that you can stand for full shifts, lift the required weight, and pass a background or drug screen if the role calls for it.

## ATS Optimization

Many employers use Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) to screen cover letters before a human reads them. To pass these filters, mirror keywords from the job description naturally throughout your letter. Avoid using headers in text boxes, tables, or graphics that ATS software cannot parse. Save your file as a PDF unless the employer specifically requests a different format.

Include the exact job title and key qualifications mentioned in the posting. If the role requires "project management experience," use that exact phrase rather than a synonym. This ensures your application scores well in automated screening while still reading naturally to hiring managers.
