If you are job hunting in Britain, the single most important document you will produce is your CV, and getting the **UK CV format** right is what separates an interview invitation from the rejection pile. British hiring conventions differ in surprising ways from the American resume, the Europass CV, and the formats used across continental Europe. A US-style one-page resume with a photo, or a CV padded with personal details, will quietly mark you out as someone unfamiliar with how UK recruiters work.

This guide explains exactly what a British CV looks like in 2026: the two-page norm, the personal statement, what you must leave off, British spelling, how to present GCSEs and A-Levels, the right-to-work note for non-UK applicants, and the most common mistakes internationals make when applying for jobs in the UK.

## What Is a UK CV and Why British Conventions Differ

A CV, short for the Latin curriculum vitae, is the standard job application document in the United Kingdom. Unlike in the United States, where CV usually means a long academic document and resume means the short business version, in Britain the word CV covers everything. Whether you are a graduate applying for your first job or an executive moving into the boardroom, you send a CV.

The defining feature of British hiring culture is a strong emphasis on equality and anti-discrimination. The Equality Act 2010 makes it unlawful to discriminate on the basis of age, race, sex, religion, disability, and other protected characteristics. As a direct result, the UK CV format deliberately strips out personal information that employers in other countries routinely expect. This single principle explains most of what surprises international applicants.

British employers and recruiters scan CVs quickly, often spending under a minute on the first pass. They are looking for a clear structure, relevant experience near the top, and evidence that you understand local norms. A CV that signals "I have applied for British jobs before" earns instant credibility.

## The Two-Page Rule for UK CVs

The most important rule of the British CV format is length: two pages of A4. This is not a loose guideline. It is the firm expectation across the overwhelming majority of UK industries, from finance and marketing to engineering and the public sector.

There are a few sensible exceptions:

- **One page** is fine for school leavers, recent graduates, and anyone with fewer than three years of experience.
- **Three pages or more** is only acceptable for academic posts, medical and clinical roles, and senior executive positions, where a longer history or a separate publications list is standard.

If your CV is spilling onto a third page, the fix is almost never to shrink the font to an unreadable size. Instead, cut older and less relevant roles to a single line, remove duties everyone already assumes, and delete filler such as "references available on request." Recruiters read a tight two-page CV far more carefully than a sprawling four-page one.

## The Personal Statement or Professional Profile

Near the top of a UK CV, just below your name and contact details, you place a short **personal statement**, sometimes called a professional profile or personal profile. This is a punchy paragraph of roughly three to four sentences, or 50 to 100 words, that summarises who you are professionally, your key strengths, and what you are looking for.

A strong personal statement is tailored to the specific role rather than generic. Compare these two openings:

- Weak: "A hardworking and motivated individual seeking a challenging new opportunity."
- Strong: "A qualified management accountant with six years' experience in FMCG, specialising in budgeting and cost control. Reduced month-end close from ten days to four and now seeking a finance business partner role in a growth-stage company."

The second version names a profession, a sector, a tangible achievement, and a clear goal. If you are unsure how to frame yours, studying a well-written [accountant CV example](/resume-examples/accountant) shows how a profile leads naturally into a finance-focused work history. Marketing applicants can see the same principle in a [marketing manager CV example](/resume-examples/marketing-manager), where the profile foregrounds campaign results and budget responsibility.

## Reverse-Chronological Work History

The standard structure for the UK CV format is reverse chronological, meaning your most recent role appears first and you work backwards through your career. British recruiters strongly prefer this layout because it lets them see your current level and trajectory at a glance. Purely skills-based or functional CVs, which hide dates, are treated with suspicion because they look like an attempt to disguise gaps or job-hopping.

For each role, include:

- Job title, employer name, and location
- Start and end dates in month and year format (e.g., March 2022 to present)
- A short line describing the company if it is not well known
- Three to six bullet points covering achievements, not just duties

Lead your bullet points with strong action verbs and quantify wherever you can. "Managed social media" says little; "Grew the company's LinkedIn following from 4,000 to 22,000 in eighteen months, driving a 30% rise in inbound leads" says a great deal. Healthcare candidates can see this achievement-led style in a [nurse CV example](/resume-examples/nurse), while technical applicants will find it in an [IT support CV example](/resume-examples/it-support-specialist) that balances ticket-resolution metrics with named systems and tools.

## What to Leave Off a UK CV

This is where international applicants most often go wrong. Because of the Equality Act 2010 and the strong British emphasis on fair recruitment, you should deliberately **omit** the following from a UK CV:

- **No photo.** A headshot is standard in Germany, France, Spain, and much of Southern Europe, but in Britain it is a liability. Many recruiters reject CVs with photos to avoid any appearance of bias during shortlisting.
- **No date of birth or age.** Age discrimination is unlawful, so your birth date has no place on the document.
- **No marital status or family details.** Whether you are married or have children is irrelevant to your ability to do the job.
- **No nationality** unless it is directly relevant to your right to work (more on this below).
- **No religion, no health information, no national insurance number, and no full home address.** A town or city plus the first part of your postcode is plenty.

Leaving these off is not an oversight that makes your CV look incomplete to a British employer. The opposite is true: including them signals that you do not understand UK norms. The same logic separates the British approach from the more detailed [Europass CV format](/blog/europass-cv-format-guide) used across the EU, which actively provides fields for nationality, date of birth, and a photo.

## British English Spelling and Terminology

A surprising number of otherwise strong applications are weakened by American spelling. UK recruiters notice, and it can read as carelessness. Use British English throughout:

- **organised**, not organized
- **specialised**, **analysed**, **recognised** (the -ise ending)
- **colour**, **favour**, **behaviour**, **labour**
- **centre**, **theatre**, **metre**
- **programme** (for a scheme or plan; "program" only for software)
- **enrolled**, **travelled**, **modelling** (double L)

Beyond spelling, use British vocabulary. Write **CV**, never resume. Write **mobile**, not cell phone. Write **university** rather than college for higher education, and **A-Levels** rather than high school diploma. Your address should follow UK conventions, and dates are best written day-month-year. Setting your word processor's language to English (UK) will catch most spelling slips automatically.

## Presenting UK Education and Qualifications

The education section of a UK CV uses qualifications that international applicants may not recognise. Listing them correctly shows you understand the British system.

- **GCSEs** are taken at around age sixteen. On a CV you typically summarise them rather than listing every subject, for example: "9 GCSEs grades 9 to 4 (A* to C), including English and Maths." The numerical 9 to 1 grading replaced the old letter grades, so either form is understood.
- **A-Levels** are taken at around age eighteen and are the main entry qualification for university. List them with grades, for example: "A-Levels: Maths (A), Economics (A), Geography (B)."
- **Degree classifications** matter a great deal to UK employers, especially for graduate schemes. List your class clearly: a **First** (1st), an **Upper Second (2:1)**, a **Lower Second (2:2)**, or a **Third**. Many graduate employers set a 2:1 as their minimum, so if you achieved one, make it prominent.

Experienced professionals can keep the education section short, placing it below work experience and summarising GCSEs in a single line. Recent graduates should place education higher up and include relevant modules, projects, or a dissertation title.

## Right to Work and Visa Notes for Non-UK Applicants

Since Brexit, right-to-work status has become a genuine concern for UK employers, who face penalties for employing someone without permission to work. If you are a non-UK applicant, a brief, factual note helps rather than hurts.

If you already hold the right to work, state it plainly near your contact details or in your personal statement, for example: "Full right to work in the UK (Settled Status / Skilled Worker visa / British citizen)." This removes a question mark before it arises. If you require sponsorship, it is honest and practical to indicate this, because many employers are licensed sponsors and many are not. This is the one circumstance where mentioning nationality or visa status on a UK CV is appropriate and expected. Outside of right-to-work relevance, leave nationality off entirely.

## UK CV vs US Resume vs Europass

Understanding how the British CV compares to other formats helps international applicants avoid sending the wrong document.

| Feature | UK CV | US Resume | Europass CV |
|--------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Length** | 2 pages | 1 page | 2 to 3 pages |
| **Photo** | No | No | Often yes |
| **Date of birth** | No | No | Optional field |
| **Spelling** | British English | American English | Varies by language |
| **Top section** | Personal statement | Summary (optional) | Personal information |
| **Layout** | Free-form | Free-form | Standardised template |

The US resume is the closest cousin, sharing the no-photo, no-date-of-birth approach, but it is shorter and uses American spelling and terminology. The Europass CV, by contrast, is a standardised European Commission template that runs longer, includes personal details the UK omits, and is rarely expected by British employers. If you are applying across Europe as well as Britain, it is worth keeping a British CV for the UK and a separate document for EU institutional roles, as explained in our guide to the [Europass CV format](/blog/europass-cv-format-guide).

## References and the "Available on Request" Line

For decades, British CVs ended with the line "References available on request." Today it is optional and increasingly seen as old-fashioned, because employers already assume references are available. On a tight two-page CV, that line is wasted space.

The modern approach is to leave it off and instead prepare a separate reference sheet listing two referees, usually a recent line manager and one other professional contact, with their permission secured in advance. Hand this over only when an employer asks, typically at the offer stage. Never publish referees' full contact details on the CV itself, both to save space and to respect their privacy.

## ATS Use Among UK Employers and Agencies

Applicant tracking systems are now standard across British recruitment. Large corporates, the NHS, the Civil Service, and virtually every recruitment agency run CVs through ATS software before a human reads them. To survive this first electronic filter:

- Use a clean, single-column layout with standard section headings such as Work Experience, Education, and Skills.
- Mirror keywords from the job description, including both full terms and abbreviations (for example "Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development (CIPD)").
- Avoid tables, text boxes, headers, footers, images, and unusual fonts, which many parsers misread.
- Save as a Word document or a text-based PDF, never as a scanned image or a heavily designed graphic file.

A clean, ATS-friendly layout also reads well to humans, so you lose nothing by following these rules. Browsing professionally structured [CV templates](/templates) is a quick way to see which designs balance ATS compatibility with a polished appearance.

## Common Mistakes Internationals Make Applying in the UK

If you are coming from the US, the EU, or further afield, these are the errors that most often trip up applicants targeting British jobs:

- **Adding a photo and personal details.** The instinct from many countries is to include a headshot, date of birth, and nationality. In Britain, all three should go.
- **Calling it a resume.** Use the word CV in your file name, your cover letter, and any email.
- **American spelling.** Set your language to English (UK) and proofread for -ize, -or, and -er endings.
- **Sending a one-page resume.** A single page reads as thin to a UK employer; aim for a full, well-used two pages.
- **Skipping the personal statement.** British recruiters expect a short profile at the top and notice when it is missing.
- **Ignoring qualification equivalence.** Translate foreign qualifications into UK terms or note the equivalent level so recruiters can place them.
- **Forgetting the cover letter.** For direct applications, especially in the public sector and education, a tailored one-page letter is still expected.

Avoiding these mistakes is mostly about adjusting to local convention rather than rewriting your experience. Once your document follows the UK CV format, your achievements can speak for themselves.
