Accounting is a credential-heavy, precision-focused field where your resume must do two things simultaneously: demonstrate your technical qualifications (GAAP, CPA, ERP systems) and show measurable financial impact through quantified achievements.

Hiring managers in finance and accounting spend considerable time on the technical skills section and look for specific numbers in every bullet point. Here is how to write an accounting resume that wins interviews at every level.

## What Accounting Hiring Managers Actually Look For

Before formatting, understand what drives hiring decisions in accounting:

**Public Accounting (Big 4, Regional Firms):**
- Specific audit, tax, or advisory practice experience
- Client industry exposure (healthcare, technology, manufacturing, etc.)
- CPA status or CPA exam progress
- Engagement sizes and complexity handled

**Corporate Accounting (Controllers, FP&A, Treasury):**
- Revenue/asset scale managed ($10M vs. $1B is a significant distinction)
- ERP systems (SAP, Oracle, NetSuite, Workday)
- Month-end close experience and cycle time
- SEC reporting for public companies; consolidations for multi-entity structures

**All levels:** Quantified everything. Accounting is about numbers — resumes that do not include them immediately read as weak.

## Accounting Resume Skills

### Technical Accounting Skills
- Financial reporting and GAAP compliance
- Month-end/quarter-end/year-end close
- General ledger maintenance and reconciliation
- Accounts payable (AP) and accounts receivable (AR)
- Budget development and variance analysis
- Audit preparation (internal and external)
- Financial statement analysis
- Tax preparation and compliance (federal, state)
- Cost accounting and standard costing
- Revenue recognition (ASC 606)
- Lease accounting (ASC 842)
- SOX compliance and internal controls

### Software and Tools
- **Accounting systems:** QuickBooks, NetSuite, SAP, Oracle Financials, Workday, Sage, Dynamics 365
- **FP&A and reporting:** Adaptive Insights, Anaplan, Hyperion, Power BI, Tableau
- **Productivity:** Excel (VLOOKUP, pivot tables, Power Query, macros), Google Sheets
- **Payroll:** ADP, Paychex, Gusto, Ceridian

### Certifications That Strengthen an Accounting Resume
- CPA (Certified Public Accountant) — most valuable in public accounting and senior corporate roles
- CMA (Certified Management Accountant) — valued in corporate FP&A and controller roles
- CIA (Certified Internal Auditor) — internal audit roles
- CFA (Chartered Financial Analyst) — investment and FP&A roles
- EA (Enrolled Agent) — tax-focused roles

## Accounting Resume Format Tips

### 1. Put CPA Credentials After Your Name
If you are a CPA, put it in the header: "Jane Smith, CPA." The designation is immediately visible and is often a hiring filter — do not bury it.

### 2. Include Revenue and Asset Scale
Every role should indicate the financial scale you operated at: "$60M revenue," "150-employee organization," "$2B bank." Size signals the complexity of your experience.

### 3. Separate Certifications From Education
Accounting has a robust certification ecosystem. Give certifications their own section rather than burying them in education. List: certification name, issuing organization, date obtained, state (for CPA), and license number if applicable.

### 4. Match ERP Systems to the Job Description
Accounting roles often require specific ERP experience (NetSuite vs. SAP vs. Oracle). Ensure the specific systems mentioned in the job description appear in your resume if you have used them.

### 5. Use Conservative Formatting
Accounting is a conservative field. Clean serif or sans-serif fonts, standard section headers, and a one-or-two-column layout are all appropriate. Avoid creative design elements — they rarely help and occasionally hurt in accounting hiring.

## Common Accounting Resume Mistakes

**1. Describing duties instead of outcomes**
"Responsible for accounts payable" → "Managed accounts payable for 180 vendors, processing $6M monthly with a 99.5% on-time payment rate."

**2. Omitting ERP and software proficiency**
Accounting is software-driven. A resume without specific system experience raises questions about technical capability.

**3. Not specifying the scale of what you managed**
"Managed the general ledger" says nothing about whether you handled $500K in a small nonprofit or $500M in a public company. Always include scale.

**4. Listing CPA as "in progress" without specifics**
Say which exams you have passed: "CPA candidate; passed FAR and REG." This shows momentum and partial qualification rather than an indefinite plan.

**5. Omitting industry context**
Healthcare accounting has different requirements than manufacturing accounting. Specify your industry experience — it is often a primary filter.

## How to Present Skills on Your Resume

Skills presentation can make or break your resume's impact. The most effective approach combines a dedicated skills section with contextual skill demonstration throughout your experience bullets.

For your skills section, organize by category: Technical Skills, Industry Tools, Certifications, and Languages. List the most relevant skills first — those matching the job description's requirements. For technical roles, include proficiency levels or years of experience with each tool.

In your experience section, demonstrate skills in action rather than simply listing them. Instead of "Proficient in Excel," write "Built automated Excel dashboards tracking $2M quarterly revenue across 5 product lines." This approach shows both the skill and its business impact.

For 2026, prioritize these high-demand skill categories:
- **AI and automation literacy** — understanding how to work with AI tools, not just use them
- **Data analysis** — interpreting data to drive decisions, regardless of your field
- **Cross-functional collaboration** — working across teams, time zones, and disciplines
- **Adaptability** — demonstrated ability to learn new tools, pivot strategies, and thrive in change

Avoid listing soft skills without evidence. "Strong communicator" means nothing without context. Instead: "Presented quarterly results to C-suite executives, translating technical metrics into actionable business insights."

## Step-by-Step Checklist

Follow this checklist to ensure your application materials are polished and competitive:

1. **Research the company** — understand their mission, products, and recent developments before tailoring your documents
2. **Match keywords** — identify the top 8-10 keywords from the job description and weave them naturally into your content
3. **Quantify achievements** — replace vague descriptions with specific numbers, percentages, and dollar amounts wherever possible
4. **Choose the right format** — chronological for steady career progression, functional for career changers, combination for experienced professionals
5. **Write a compelling summary** — your professional summary should be a 3-4 sentence elevator pitch that hooks the reader
6. **Proofread thoroughly** — read your document backwards sentence by sentence to catch errors your brain normally autocorrects
7. **Get a second opinion** — ask a trusted colleague or mentor to review your final draft for clarity and impact
8. **Save in the right format** — PDF preserves formatting across all devices; DOCX only when specifically requested
9. **Follow up strategically** — send a brief follow-up email 5-7 business days after applying if you have not heard back
10. **Track your applications** — maintain a spreadsheet with company names, dates, contacts, and status to stay organized

This systematic approach ensures nothing falls through the cracks during your job search. Consistency and attention to detail set successful candidates apart from the competition.
