A portfolio is your proof of work. Where your resume describes what you have done, a portfolio shows it. For creative and technical roles, a strong portfolio often matters more than the resume itself — it is what hiring managers click on first and remember longest.

Here is how to build, host, and link a professional portfolio that strengthens your application.

## Which Roles Need a Portfolio

### Essential (hiring managers expect it)
- Graphic designer
- UX/UI designer
- Web developer / frontend engineer
- Data scientist / data analyst
- Video editor / motion designer
- Photographer / videographer
- Architect
- Writer / content strategist / copywriter
- Illustrator / animator

### Strongly recommended
- Marketing manager (campaign examples, results)
- Product manager (case studies, product thinking)
- UX researcher (research reports, insights presentations)
- Software engineer (GitHub profile with active, documented projects)
- Social media manager (content examples, account metrics)
- Instructional designer (course samples, e-learning modules)

### Optional but differentiating
- Project manager (documented case studies of complex projects)
- Data engineer (GitHub with data pipeline projects)
- Operations manager (process documentation, results summaries)
- Sales manager (deal case studies, team performance results)

If you are in any of these roles, adding a portfolio is one of the highest-leverage resume improvements you can make.

## What to Include in Your Portfolio

### The Right Number of Projects

**3-6 projects** is the sweet spot. Less than 3 looks thin; more than 10 requires curation decisions that many candidates defer, resulting in an unfocused portfolio that dilutes your strongest work.

### Case Studies, Not Just Outputs

The most common portfolio mistake: showing only the final product without explaining the process that created it. Hiring managers want to understand how you think — not just what you can produce.

A strong case study includes:
1. **The challenge or objective** — What problem were you solving? For whom?
2. **Your role** — What specifically did you contribute?
3. **Your process** — What tools, methods, or frameworks did you use?
4. **Key decisions** — What choices did you make and why?
5. **Results** — What was the measurable outcome?
6. **The final work** — The actual deliverable

**Example case study outline (UX designer):**

*Project: Redesign of onboarding flow for a mobile banking app*

**Challenge:** 43% of new users abandoned during the 6-step onboarding flow. Product analytics showed the largest drop-off at step 4 (identity verification).

**My role:** Lead UX researcher and designer on a 3-person team.

**Process:** Conducted 8 user interviews to identify friction points; created journey maps highlighting emotional friction; designed 3 alternative flows; ran 2 rounds of usability testing (5 participants each).

**Key decision:** Simplified the verification step from 3 separate screens to a single scrollable screen — a counterintuitive choice given the complexity of the content, but validated by testing.

**Result:** Onboarding completion rate improved from 57% to 81% in the 6 weeks following launch.

**Final work:** [Images of the redesigned screens, prototype link]

---

This case study is 10x more compelling than a portfolio that only shows the final screen designs.

### Selecting Your Best Projects

When choosing which projects to feature, prioritize:

1. **Impact** — Projects with measurable results (revenue, metrics, user outcomes) beat technically impressive work with no measurable impact
2. **Recency** — Work from the past 3-5 years is most relevant; very old work suggests you have not been active
3. **Relevance to target role** — Match your portfolio to your target job type; a designer targeting e-commerce should feature e-commerce projects prominently
4. **Variety** — Show range across deliverable types (branding, digital, print, motion) if relevant to your field
5. **Personal interest** — Passion projects often show your best work, especially for creative roles

### What to Include for Specific Roles

**Graphic Designer:** Brand identity systems, packaging, editorial, print, digital campaigns — show range but curate to your strongest

**UX/UI Designer:** End-to-end case studies showing research → wireframes → prototypes → final designs; Figma prototypes are highly valued

**Software Developer:** GitHub with documented repositories, README files, deployed applications, open-source contributions

**Data Scientist/Analyst:** Jupyter notebooks or Python scripts with documented analysis, Tableau/Looker Public dashboards, write-ups explaining methodology and findings

**Writer/Content Strategist:** Published articles, content campaigns, brand voice guides, editorial calendars; organized by content type or client

**Product Manager:** Written case studies documenting product problems, your research, decisions made, trade-offs, and outcomes — this is primarily a writing exercise

## Where to Host Your Portfolio

### By Role

| Role | Best Portfolio Platform |
|------|------------------------|
| Graphic Designer | Behance, Cargo, personal domain |
| UX/UI Designer | UX Folio, Dribbble, Behance, personal site |
| Web Developer | GitHub Pages, personal domain |
| Data Scientist | GitHub, Kaggle, Google Colab |
| Photographer | Format, SmugMug, personal domain |
| Writer | Contently, Muck Rack, personal blog |
| Video Editor | Vimeo, YouTube (unlisted or public) |
| Freelancer (general) | Contra, Fiverr portfolio |
| Any role | Notion (modern, flexible, free) |

### Platform Comparison

**Behance (free):** Best for visual designers; large creative community; built-in discoverability

**GitHub (free):** Essential for developers and data scientists; shows real code and project history

**Personal domain:** Most professional and customizable; requires setup; worth it for senior roles

**Notion (free):** Flexible for any type of portfolio; especially good for PM, writer, or operations case studies

**Squarespace / Wix (paid):** Best for visual presentation when design quality of the site itself matters

**Format (paid):** Beautiful, photography-optimized galleries

### Technical Requirements for Your Portfolio Site

Whatever platform you use, ensure:
- Loads in under 3 seconds — slow load = abandoned portfolio
- Mobile-responsive — many hiring managers view on phone
- Clear navigation — they should be able to find your best project within 30 seconds
- Contact or link back to LinkedIn — make it easy to follow up
- Case study depth — at least 2-3 projects with full case studies, not just images

## How to Link Your Portfolio from Your Resume

### In the Header
Add the URL next to your email, phone, and LinkedIn:

```
JANE SMITH
jane.smith@gmail.com | (555) 234-5678 | Brooklyn, NY
linkedin.com/in/janesmith | portfolio.janesmith.com
```

For roles where the portfolio is essential (design, development), make it as prominent as LinkedIn.

### In Bullet Points
For specific projects, link to the relevant case study directly (in digital PDF format):

```
• Redesigned mobile onboarding flow, improving completion rate from 57% to 81%
  [View case study: portfolio.janesmith.com/onboarding]
```

### As a Featured Link
Some candidates add a brief line in their Professional Summary:

```
Portfolio: portfolio.janesmith.com | GitHub: github.com/janesmith
```

This format works well for technical candidates and designers who want the link highly visible.

## Before You Link It: Portfolio Checklist

Before adding any portfolio URL to your resume or LinkedIn, confirm:

- [ ] **Loads correctly** — Test on Chrome, Safari, and mobile
- [ ] **URL is clean and memorable** — yourname.com, not squarespace-generated-gibberish.com
- [ ] **Best work is prominent** — Your strongest project should be the first thing they see
- [ ] **Case studies are complete** — Context, process, and results are all there
- [ ] **Contact information is present** — Email or a contact form
- [ ] **Old or irrelevant work is removed** — Student projects from 2015 do not belong unless you have nothing else
