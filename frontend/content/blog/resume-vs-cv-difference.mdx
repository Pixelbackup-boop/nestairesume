"Should I submit a resume or a CV?" This question confuses job seekers worldwide — [Indeed](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/resumes-cover-letters/difference-between-resume-and-cv) defines the distinction based on length, purpose, and geography — partly because the terms mean different things depending on where you live. In the US, they're two distinct documents. In the UK, "CV" is what Americans call a "resume."

Here's a clear, definitive breakdown.

## Resume vs CV: Quick Comparison

| Feature | Resume | CV (Curriculum Vitae) |
|---------|--------|----------------------|
| **Length** | 1-2 pages | No limit (2-20+ pages) |
| **Content** | Tailored to specific job | Complete career history |
| **Purpose** | Get a job interview | Document academic career |
| **Customized?** | Yes, for each application | No, one master document |
| **Includes publications?** | No | Yes |
| **Used where?** | Most industries (US/Canada) | Academia, research, medicine |
| **Changes over time** | Condensed as you gain experience | Grows longer with every achievement |

## What Is a Resume?

A resume (from French *résumé*, meaning "summary") is a **concise, tailored document** designed to land you an interview for a specific job.

### Key characteristics:
- **1-2 pages maximum** (1 page for early career, 2 for 10+ years experience)
- **Customized** for each job application — you emphasize different skills and achievements
- **Results-focused** — uses metrics and accomplishments, not just job duties
- **Reverse chronological** — most recent experience first
- **Keyword-optimized** — tailored to pass Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS)

### Resume sections:
1. Contact information
2. Professional summary or objective
3. Work experience (with achievements)
4. Skills
5. Education
6. Certifications (optional)

### A resume is for:
- Corporate jobs
- Tech, marketing, finance, sales roles
- Startups and small businesses
- Most non-academic positions in the US and Canada

## What Is a CV (Curriculum Vitae)?

A CV (Latin for "course of life") is a **comprehensive document** that details your entire academic and professional history. Unlike a resume, it's not tailored — it's a complete record that grows over your career.

### Key characteristics:
- **No page limit** — grows with your career (professors may have 15-20 page CVs)
- **Comprehensive** — includes everything relevant to your academic career
- **Not customized** per application — one master document
- **Chronological** — organized by category (publications, teaching, grants)
- **Detail-oriented** — lists every paper, presentation, grant, and committee

### CV sections:
1. Contact information
2. Research interests
3. Education
4. Academic positions held
5. Publications (peer-reviewed, books, chapters)
6. Conference presentations
7. Grants and fellowships
8. Teaching experience
9. Awards and honors
10. Professional memberships
11. Service and committee work
12. References

### A CV is for:
- University faculty positions
- Research scientist roles
- Medical residencies and fellowships
- PhD program applications
- Federal government positions (some)
- International job applications (UK, Europe, Australia)

## The International Confusion

The biggest source of confusion: **the word "CV" means different things in different countries.**

| Country | "Resume" means... | "CV" means... |
|---------|-------------------|---------------|
| **USA / Canada** | Short job application (1-2 pages) | Long academic document (no page limit) |
| **UK / Ireland** | Not commonly used | Short job application (1-2 pages) — same as US "resume" |
| **Australia / NZ** | Short job application | Same as resume (used interchangeably) |
| **Europe** | Less common term | Short job application (like US resume) |
| **Middle East / Asia** | Used interchangeably | Used interchangeably |

**Practical tip:** When applying to a UK, Australian, or European company that asks for a "CV," they want a short, tailored document — essentially what Americans call a resume. Don't send a 10-page academic CV.

## When to Use a Resume

Use a resume when:
- The job posting says "submit your resume"
- You're applying to a **non-academic** position in the US or Canada
- The role is in corporate, tech, healthcare (non-research), finance, retail, or services
- You're applying through an ATS or online portal (ATS expects concise documents)
- The posting doesn't specify — resume is the safe default in North America

## When to Use a CV

Use a CV when:
- The job posting specifically asks for a "curriculum vitae"
- You're applying for a **faculty, research, or postdoctoral** position
- You're applying to a **medical residency or fellowship**
- The role requires listing publications, grants, or research
- You're applying for a **federal government** job (some agencies prefer CVs)
- You're applying **internationally** and the posting says "CV" (but check the country context)

## How to Convert a CV to a Resume (and Vice Versa)

### CV → Resume (condensing)
1. Cut everything below 2 pages
2. Remove publications, presentations, and committee lists
3. Add a professional summary (CVs don't have these)
4. Rewrite academic achievements as business-impact bullet points
5. Tailor content to the specific job description
6. Add a skills section with industry keywords

### Resume → CV (expanding)
1. Remove the professional summary (CVs don't use them)
2. Add comprehensive sections: publications, research, teaching, grants
3. Include ALL relevant academic activities, not just highlights
4. Add conference presentations with full citations
5. Include professional memberships and service
6. Don't worry about page count — completeness matters more

## When to Use a CV vs a Resume by Industry

The resume-vs-CV decision isn't just about geography -- certain industries have strong norms regardless of where you're located.

| Industry | Document | Why |
|----------|----------|-----|
| **Software / Tech** | Resume | Fast hiring cycles; recruiters want concise proof of technical skills, not publication lists |
| **Investment Banking** | Resume (1 page only) | One of the most page-count-rigid industries; even MDs use 1 page |
| **Academic Research** | CV | Search committees expect a complete record of publications, grants, and teaching |
| **Medical (Clinical)** | Resume or CV | Nurses and allied health use resumes; physicians applying for faculty or research positions use CVs |
| **Medical (Residency)** | CV | Residency applications through ERAS require a full CV with research, volunteer work, and publications |
| **Federal Government (US)** | Federal resume (hybrid) | USAJobs requires a detailed format -- longer than a resume but more structured than an academic CV |
| **Consulting** | Resume | McKinsey, BCG, and Bain all expect 1-page resumes for experienced hires and MBA applicants |
| **Creative / Design** | Resume + portfolio | Keep the document short; let your portfolio website showcase your actual work |
| **Nonprofit / NGO** | Resume (1-2 pages) | Similar to corporate format; international NGOs sometimes request a CV in the European sense |
| **Law** | Resume | Most law firms expect a standard resume; judicial clerkship applications may require a CV |

If you're unsure, read the job posting carefully. Phrases like "submit your CV" from a UK-based company mean a short resume. Phrases like "submit a curriculum vitae including publications" from a US university mean a full academic CV.

## CV and Resume Length Guidelines by Career Stage

How long each document should be depends heavily on where you are in your career:

### Resume Length by Career Stage

- **Entry-level (0-2 years):** Strictly 1 page. Include education, internships, projects, and relevant coursework. Anything longer signals you can't prioritize.
- **Mid-career (3-10 years):** 1 page preferred, 2 pages acceptable if every line adds value. Focus on achievements from the last 2-3 roles.
- **Senior / Executive (10+ years):** 2 pages. Lead with a strong executive summary, highlight leadership scope, and quantify business impact (revenue, team size, budget).
- **Career changer:** 1-2 pages. Use the space to bridge your old experience to the new field with transferable skills and relevant certifications.

### CV Length by Career Stage

- **PhD student / Postdoc:** 2-5 pages. Include dissertation title, research interests, publications (even if in review), conference presentations, and teaching assistantships.
- **Assistant Professor:** 5-10 pages. Full publication list, all grants (funded and unfunded), courses taught, graduate students supervised, and committee service.
- **Full Professor / Department Chair:** 10-20+ pages. Decades of publications, keynote invitations, editorial board memberships, and institutional leadership. Never cut content from an academic CV -- it's a living record.
- **Medical Resident / Fellow:** 3-6 pages. Clinical rotations, research experience, publications, presentations, volunteer work, and medical licenses.

The cardinal rule for resumes: if it doesn't help you get an interview for *this specific job*, cut it. The cardinal rule for CVs: if it's a legitimate academic or professional accomplishment, include it.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Sending a CV for a corporate job** — You'll look like you don't understand the industry
2. **Sending a resume for an academic job** — You'll look unqualified (where are your publications?)
3. **Confusing UK "CV" with US "CV"** — A British employer asking for a CV wants 2 pages, not 20
4. **Making your resume too long** — If you're not in academia, keep it to 1-2 pages
5. **Not tailoring your resume** — CVs are one-size-fits-all; resumes must be customized per application
