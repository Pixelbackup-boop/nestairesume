Every professional started with zero experience. [NACE](https://www.naceweb.org/) research shows employers hiring students value potential over tenure. The challenge of writing your first resume is real — but the solution is simpler than you think. Employers hiring students and interns **expect** limited work history. What they're looking for is **potential**: relevant skills, academic performance, initiative, and adaptability.

Here's how to build a resume that gets interviews even without a career to reference.

## Student Resume Structure (Recommended Order)

Since you can't lead with work experience, reorder your resume sections:

1. **Contact Information**
2. **Resume Objective** (not a summary — you don't have enough experience for one)
3. **Education** (this is your strongest section)
4. **Projects** (academic, personal, or freelance)
5. **Experience** (internships, part-time jobs, volunteer work)
6. **Skills** (technical and soft)
7. **Activities & Leadership** (clubs, sports, organizations)

## Section-by-Section Guide

### 1. Contact Information

Keep it professional:
- Full name
- Phone number
- Professional email (firstname.lastname@gmail.com, not xXgamer99Xx@yahoo.com)
- LinkedIn profile URL (customized)
- Portfolio or GitHub link (if applicable)

**Don't include:** Home address (outdated practice), photo (in the US), date of birth.

### 2. Resume Objective

Students should use an **objective**, not a summary. You're stating your goal and what you bring to the table.

**College junior seeking internship:**
> "Computer science junior at Georgia Tech with a 3.7 GPA seeking a summer software engineering internship. Experienced in Python, Java, and React through coursework and 3 personal projects. Eager to apply machine learning knowledge in a production environment."

**Recent graduate seeking first job:**
> "Recent B.S. Marketing graduate from ASU with Google Analytics and HubSpot certifications. Seeking an entry-level Digital Marketing Coordinator role to apply data-driven campaign management skills developed through 2 agency internships."

**High school student:**
> "Motivated high school senior with a 3.5 GPA and 200+ hours of community service seeking a part-time retail position. Strong communication and teamwork skills developed through varsity debate team captain role."

### 3. Education

For students, education goes **above** experience. Include:

```
EDUCATION
B.S. Computer Science | Georgia Tech | Expected May 2027
GPA: 3.7/4.0 | Dean's List (4 semesters)
Relevant Coursework: Data Structures, Machine Learning, Database Systems, Software Engineering
```

**What to include:**
- Degree name and major
- University name and expected graduation date
- GPA (if 3.0+) — major GPA if higher than cumulative
- Dean's List, academic honors, or scholarships
- Relevant coursework (4-6 courses that match the job)
- Study abroad (shows initiative and adaptability)

**What to skip:**
- High school (if you're in college, unless it's a very prestigious school or you have no college activities yet)
- GPA below 3.0 (just leave it off)
- Irrelevant coursework (English 101 won't help your engineering application)

### 4. Projects

This is where students without work experience can shine. Include:

**Academic projects:**
> **E-Commerce Database System** | Database Systems Course | Fall 2025
> - Designed and implemented a PostgreSQL database with 15 tables supporting 10,000+ product records
> - Built REST API using Node.js and Express with authentication and rate limiting
> - Deployed on AWS EC2 with 99.9% uptime during 3-week testing period

**Personal projects:**
> **Personal Finance Tracker** | React, Firebase, Chart.js | 2025
> - Built a full-stack budgeting app with real-time expense tracking and visual spending analytics
> - Implemented Google OAuth and bank account sync using Plaid API
> - Gained 150+ users after sharing on Reddit's r/personalfinance

**Hackathon projects:**
> **MealMatch App** | HackGT 2025 — 2nd Place | React Native, GPT API
> - Developed a meal planning app that generates recipes based on available ingredients using AI
> - Led a 3-person team through design, development, and pitch in 36 hours

### 5. Experience

Include **anything** where you worked — even if it seems unrelated:

**Internship (best case):**
> **Marketing Intern** | Startup XYZ | Summer 2025
> - Managed social media accounts growing Instagram from 2K to 8K followers in 3 months
> - Created 30+ pieces of content using Canva and Adobe Premiere
> - Analyzed campaign performance in Google Analytics, presenting weekly reports to the CEO

**Part-time job (reframed):**
> **Barista** | Starbucks | Sep 2024 – Present
> - Serve 200+ customers daily in a high-volume location, maintaining speed and quality standards
> - Train 5 new hires on POS system, drink recipes, and customer service protocols
> - Recognized as "Partner of the Quarter" for highest customer satisfaction scores

**Volunteer work:**
> **Web Developer Volunteer** | Habitat for Humanity — Local Chapter | 2025
> - Redesigned chapter website using WordPress, increasing donation page visits by 40%
> - Integrated a Stripe payment form that processed $12K in online donations during first quarter

### 6. Skills

Split into categories for clarity:

```
SKILLS
Technical: Python, Java, JavaScript, React, SQL, Git, AWS, Figma
Tools: VS Code, Jira, Google Analytics, Tableau, Microsoft Office
Languages: English (native), Spanish (conversational), Mandarin (basic)
Certifications: Google Data Analytics Certificate, AWS Cloud Practitioner
```

### 7. Activities & Leadership

Clubs, sports, and organizations show initiative:

```
ACTIVITIES
- President, Computer Science Club (2025-2026) — Organized 12 tech talks with 80+ attendance
- Varsity Tennis Team — Balanced 20hr/week training with full course load (3.7 GPA)
- Google Developer Student Club Member — Completed 3 collaborative open-source projects
```

## Common Student Resume Mistakes

1. **Leaving the page half-empty** — If you can't fill a page, add coursework, projects, and skills sections
2. **Including every job since age 14** — Only include positions relevant to your target role (or recent ones)
3. **Generic objective** — "Seeking a challenging position" tells the employer nothing
4. **Missing keywords** — Even entry-level jobs use ATS. Include keywords from the job posting
5. **Unprofessional email** — Get a firstname.lastname@gmail.com address
6. **Including "References available upon request"** — This wastes space and is assumed

## Student Resume by Year

| Year | Lead With | Include | Remove |
|------|-----------|---------|--------|
| **Freshman** | Education, HS achievements | Volunteer work, skills, clubs | — |
| **Sophomore** | Education, coursework | Part-time jobs, projects, clubs | HS achievements |
| **Junior** | Education, projects | Internship, technical skills | Unrelated part-time jobs |
| **Senior/New Grad** | Education, experience | Internships, capstone, portfolio | High school entirely |

## Best Sections for a Student Resume

Not every resume section makes sense for students. Knowing what to include and what to skip prevents your resume from looking padded or unfocused.

### Sections Every Student Resume Should Have
- **Contact Information**: Name, phone, email, LinkedIn, and portfolio or GitHub if applicable
- **Resume Objective**: A clear statement of your goal and what you bring (not a professional summary; you do not have enough experience for one yet)
- **Education**: Your strongest section. Degree, GPA if 3.0+, honors, and relevant coursework
- **Skills**: Technical tools, programming languages, software, and certifications organized by category

### Sections to Add Based on Your Situation
- **Projects**: Essential if you lack work experience. Academic, personal, or hackathon projects demonstrate applied skills
- **Experience**: Include internships, part-time jobs, volunteer work, and campus employment. Even unrelated roles show work ethic when properly framed
- **Activities and Leadership**: Club officer roles, sports team participation, and organizations demonstrate initiative and time management

### Sections to Skip
- **Professional Summary**: This is for people with 2+ years of career accomplishments. Students should use an objective instead
- **References**: Never include "References available upon request." It wastes space and is universally assumed
- **Hobbies and Interests**: Only include these if they are directly relevant (e.g., "competitive programming" for a software role) or you need to fill space on an otherwise thin resume
- **High School Information**: Remove once you have any college content, unless you are a freshman with no college activities yet

## How to List Coursework and Academic Projects

Coursework and projects replace the work experience that students lack. Formatting them correctly makes them look professional rather than like filler.

### Relevant Coursework

List 4-6 courses that directly relate to your target role. Use the course topic, not the course number:

```
Relevant Coursework: Data Structures & Algorithms, Machine Learning,
Database Systems, Software Engineering, Computer Networks, Operating Systems
```

Do not list general education courses (English Composition, Calculus I) unless the role specifically requires them. The purpose is to show you have academic preparation for the job, not to display your full transcript.

### Academic Projects

Format academic projects like work experience entries with a title, context, and achievement-focused bullet points:

```
Sentiment Analysis Pipeline | Machine Learning Course | Spring 2026
- Built a Python pipeline using scikit-learn to classify 50,000 product
  reviews with 89% accuracy
- Implemented data preprocessing, feature engineering, and model
  comparison (Naive Bayes, SVM, Random Forest)
- Presented findings to a class of 40 students, receiving highest
  project grade in the section
```

Three elements make a project entry effective: the technology used, the scale or scope of the work, and a measurable outcome (accuracy percentage, users reached, grade earned, competition placement).

### Capstone and Thesis Projects

Senior capstone projects and undergraduate theses deserve more space than regular coursework projects. These demonstrate sustained effort, independent thinking, and deeper technical ability. Include the advisor's name if they are well-known in the field, and link to the project repository or paper if available.

## Extracurricular Activities That Impress Employers

Not all extracurriculars carry equal weight on a resume. Employers look for activities that demonstrate transferable professional skills.

### High-Impact Activities
- **Club leadership roles** (President, VP, Treasurer): Managing people, budgets, and events translates directly to workplace skills. "Managed a $5,000 annual budget for the Computer Science Club" is a real accomplishment
- **Competitive teams** (debate, hackathons, case competitions): Competing under pressure and delivering results on deadlines mirrors professional environments
- **Research assistantships**: Working with a professor shows analytical rigor, self-direction, and the ability to contribute to a team
- **Volunteer work with measurable impact**: "Tutored 15 students weekly in calculus, with 90% passing the final exam" is far stronger than "Volunteered as a tutor"

### Medium-Impact Activities
- **General club membership**: Being a member of the Marketing Club is less impressive than holding a role, but it shows interest in your field
- **Varsity or club sports**: Demonstrates discipline and time management, especially when paired with a strong GPA
- **Student government**: Shows organizational and communication skills

### Low-Impact Activities (Include Only If Needed)
- **Greek life membership** without a leadership role: Fraternity or sorority membership alone does not differentiate you
- **Recreational clubs** with no leadership or achievement: "Member of Hiking Club" fills space but adds little professional signal
- **Religious or political organizations**: These can be polarizing. Include only if the role demonstrates transferable skills and you are comfortable with the impression

## Student Resume Mistakes That Cost You Interviews

Beyond the common mistakes listed earlier, students make several field-specific errors that experienced professionals would never make.

### Using a College Email Address After Graduation
Your .edu email may deactivate after you leave. Switch to a professional Gmail or personal domain email before you start applying, and update it on all application materials.

### Listing Every Course You Have Taken
Recruiters do not need to see your full academic transcript on your resume. List only courses that relate to the target job. Six courses is the maximum; four is ideal.

### Overvaluing GPA and Undervaluing Experience
A 4.0 GPA is impressive, but a 3.5 GPA paired with an internship and two projects is more competitive for most roles. Do not spend half your resume on academic honors at the expense of demonstrating applied skills.

### Formatting Academic Projects as Paragraphs Instead of Bullet Points
Projects formatted as dense paragraphs are skipped by recruiters scanning quickly. Use the same bullet-point format you would use for work experience: action verb, what you did, and a measurable result.

### Ignoring ATS Keywords for Entry-Level Roles
Students often assume ATS optimization only matters for experienced professionals. In reality, many entry-level and internship postings use ATS filters. Read the job description, identify required skills and tools, and mirror that exact language in your resume.

### Sending the Same Resume to Every Application
Each job posting emphasizes different skills and qualifications. Tailoring your objective, skills section, and project descriptions to match each posting increases your ATS match score and shows recruiters you read the job description carefully.
